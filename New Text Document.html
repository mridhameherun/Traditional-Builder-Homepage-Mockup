01The specialism

Churches and listed buildings aren't a sideline. They're a different trade.

Plenty of builders in this valley will happily quote your extension. Ask them to repoint a tower seventy foot up, match stone to a five-hundred-year-old wall, or work to a faculty and a conservation officer's spec — and the quotes quietly stop coming back. That's the work we're known for, and it's why church committees as far away as Skipton ring a firm from Fenwick.

i.

Faculty and listed-building consent handled start to finish — the PCC deals with one builder, not four.

ii.

Lime mortar and traditional build — not cement patches that hold water in and rot a wall from the inside.

iii.

Stone sourced and cut to match what's there. Not the nearest thing on a shelf.

iv.

Live churches stay live — services, weddings and funerals uninterrupted while we work.

Typical heritage contracts: £18k for a louvre repair up to £250k for a full tower programme.

01

St Bartholomew's tower

EcclesiasticalColnebridgeBand £90k–£120k11 weeks on site

The problem

The five-yearly survey failed the tower. Belfry louvres rotted through, parapet stone spalling, and the pointing gone to powder wherever cement had been patched in during the seventies.

The work

Eleven weeks up the scaffold. Louvres rebuilt in English oak, the parapet taken down stone by stone and rebuilt with new blocks cut to match the old, and the whole elevation repointed in lime. We handled the faculty end to end, so the PCC dealt with one builder, not four.

The result

Handed back on the date in the programme, inside the agreed band. The inspecting architect's follow-up came back with no remedials, and the bells rang the Sunday after scaffold-down.

02

The Old Lock House

Renovation & extensionFenwickBand £110k–£150k5 months

The problem

An 1850s weaver's cottage: a kitchen the size of a table, damp climbing two walls, and a back yard the family crossed the house to avoid.

The work

Cause first — two failed lintels replaced and the damp traced to its source and dealt with, not painted over. Then a single-storey extension in stone reclaimed from a demolished outbuilding, giving a kitchen and family room that open onto the yard.

The result

Dry through two winters since, and the extension sits so comfortably in the terrace that it reads as original from the pavement.

03

The Star & Garter

Commercial fit-outMarsh EndBand £80k–£110k9 weeks

The problem

A village pub with a beer garden the kitchen couldn't serve. Tired equipment, a layout that blocked the pass, and an environmental health report that read like a shopping list.

The work

A nine-week refit at the back end of the season: new bar, cellar works, and a full stainless kitchen laid out in the order chefs actually work — fridge to pass to wash-up. The garden stayed open throughout so trade kept ticking.

The result

Reopened on the advertised date and cleared the re-inspection at first visit. Friday covers now run at three times what the old kitchen managed.

04

Mill Yard Mews

New build · two dwellingsColnebridgeBand £380k–£450k each14 months

The problem

A backland plot behind a row of mill cottages — awkward access, a right of way to protect, and a planning brief that asked for two homes "that belong to the street".

The work

Two three-bed houses faced in stone reclaimed from the mill outbuilding that stood on the plot — groundworks, drainage, structure and fit-out under one contract, one site manager, one point of contact.

The result

Finished inside fourteen months with no planning hiccups at sign-off, and the street gained two houses that look like they were always there.

05The family

One name on the van since 1998.

Hartley Building Contractors was started by Michael Hartley in 1998 — one Transit, one mixer and a stonemason's apprenticeship behind him, working out of a lock-up in Fenwick. The first jobs were pointing and garden walls. In 2004 a leaking vestry roof at St Bartholomew's put the firm on the diocesan list, and heritage work has been the backbone of it since. Michael's son Tom joined in 2012 after four years with a conservation firm in York, and the two run the jobs together now — one on the tools, one on the paperwork, both reachable on a bad day.

1998

Michael starts the firm. One van, one mixer, pointing and garden walls.

2004

First church contract: the vestry roof at St Bartholomew's. It led to the next one, and the one after that.

2012

Tom Hartley joins full-time, four years of conservation experience in York behind him.

2018

The yard moves to purpose-built premises just outside Fenwick. Eleven on the books, all directly employed.

Second generation, same standards. Michael runs the heritage jobs, Tom the rest.
